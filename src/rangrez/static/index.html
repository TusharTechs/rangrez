    A dye house's lab dip, in minutes instead of days, from a phone.

    Before a garment order is dyed, the dye house dyes a small swatch, a lab dip, and sends it to the buyer for approval. A rejected dip means correct, re-dye, re-send: usually 2 to 4 rounds of several days each. Large mills shorten this with spectrophotometers and colour-matching software. The thousands of small dye houses and job workers in India's textile towns mostly rely on a colourist's eye and repeated trials.

    Rangrez runs the colourist's loop itself: pick a recipe, dye a strip, photograph it, measure it, correct it, until the cloth matches. Six Meta models each do one job in that loop.

    Six Meta models, six real jobs

    SAM 3.1
    A measuring instrument: segments the colour card, the white paper and the dyed strip in every photo, so pixels become calibrated CIELAB.
    Muse Wonder
    The colourist: runs each campaign through tools (suggest, dip, refit, escalate) and writes the lab notebook. It never drives a pump directly.
    Muse Glimmer 30B
    Open weights, fine-tuned on the lab's own dips to propose the first recipe; v2 reads the lab's notebook. Weights published.
    Muse Spark
    Reads the buyer's chat message (English or Hindi) into an order, sets the pass tolerance, and drafts the reply in the buyer's language.
    Muse Image
    Shows the customer's garment in the approved shade; SAM measures and corrects it, because an image model is not a colour instrument.
    Muse Voice Transcribe
    Hands-free for a dyer with wet gloves: "done", "repeat", "stop", or "हो गया", "फिर से", "बंद करो".

    Where it stands, honestly

    • Simulated, not yet dyed for real. The physics, photos and chemistry are simulated. The first real campaign, household colours on a cotton strip with a phone, is the next step.
    • The robot rig is designed, not built. Firmware, wiring and a virtual ESP32 that runs the real driver are done; the parts are about ₹8,000.
    • A camera is a pre-screen, not a certificate. ±0.7 ΔE00 with the card is good enough to correct fast, not to certify a strict 1.0 pass; it cannot check metamerism. Final approval stays with the buyer's instrument or light booth.
    • A lab dip must follow the bulk process. Fixing, rinsing and soaping are in the protocol; transferring a recipe to bulk still needs the dye house's own lab-to-bulk factor.
    • Household colours are for learning and play. Food colours and inks are not wash-fast; dye houses use reactive dyes.
    • Glimmer is not magic. On the same data a Gaussian process matches it; Glimmer earns its place by reading the lab's notebook and working with language.

    Why it can work

    Technically
    Runs on a laptop and a phone; the measuring, chemistry and safety are plain tested code, and the Meta models do what they are best at. Every campaign is recorded and replayable.
    For the business
    Each avoided round saves days of a buyer's lead time and a courier; the report is the document the buyer already asks for. Per-dip or monthly pricing for small dye houses and sourcing agents.
    For the people using it
    Phone first, Hindi, hands-free, WhatsApp-shaped. No new instrument to learn: photograph, dip, send.
    The adoption path
    1) assisted mode with a phone, zero capex; 2) the $100 rig for unattended dips; 3) a handheld spectrophotometer in place of the camera for strict shades, same agent and memory.

    What it could become

    • Real-dye pilots with job-work dye houses in Tiruppur and Erode.
    • Spectral camera calibration, and profiles for polyester (disperse dyes) and wool.
    • Glimmer reading the dip photo itself, not just numbers.
    • Lab memory shared across dye houses that use the same dye lots, without sharing their recipes.
